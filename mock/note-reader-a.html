Thermal equilibrium & temperature scales

What temperature actually is, how the three scales relate, and where the kinetic picture comes from.

15 Oct 2026 20 min read 1,840 words

Heat always moves from hot to cold — but what does hot mean, exactly? This note builds temperature from the ground up: the zeroth law, the three everyday scales, and the kinetic picture that ties them to molecular speeds.

Temperature is the state variable that decides the direction of spontaneous heat flow. Nothing more, nothing less.

The idea in one line

01Temperature & thermal equilibrium

Two objects in contact exchange energy until neither has any net flow left. At that point they are in thermal equilibrium, and we say they share a temperature. The zeroth law turns that observation into a measuring rule:

If A and C are each in equilibrium with B, they are in equilibrium with each other — so B can serve as a thermometer.

For a dilute ideal gas, temperature is proportional to the mean translational kinetic energy per molecule:

kB = 1.381 × 10−23 J/K. Temperature is an average, not a total: a cup of tea and a bathtub can share a temperature while holding very different internal energy.

02Temperature scales

Every scale needs two fixed, reproducible points. Celsius uses the ice and steam points of water at 1 atm; Kelvin shifts the zero to absolute zero.

ScaleReference pointsConversion to K
Celsius °C0 °C ice melt · 100 °C boil
Fahrenheit °F32 °F · 212 °F
Kelvin KAbsolute zero = 0 K

Only Kelvin is absolute: is where translational motion stops, so ratios of temperatures are meaningful only in kelvin.

Why do the gas laws need kelvin?

NotezAi

Because they are proportional statements. only holds when is measured from absolute zero — at 0 °C a gas still has motion, so Celsius would make the ratio collapse at an arbitrary point.

Kelvin also keeps the kinetic law honest: has to reach zero when the motion does.
