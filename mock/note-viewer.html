Thermal equilibrium & temperature scales

How temperature is defined, how the scales relate, and where the kinetic model comes from.

Physics 20 min read Updated 15 Oct 2026 1,840 words

Heat always moves from hot to cold — but what does hot mean, exactly? This note builds temperature from the ground up: the zeroth law, the three everyday scales, and the kinetic picture that connects them to molecular speeds.

Key idea

Temperature is the state variable that decides the direction of spontaneous heat flow. Nothing more, nothing less — it is defined by equilibrium, not by a thermometer.

01Temperature & thermal equilibrium

Two objects in contact exchange energy until neither has any net flow left. At that point they are in thermal equilibrium, and we say they share a temperature. The zeroth law turns that observation into a measuring rule:

If A and C are each in equilibrium with B, they are in equilibrium with each other — so B can act as a thermometer.

For a dilute ideal gas, temperature is proportional to the mean translational kinetic energy per molecule:

kB = 1.381 × 10−23 J/K. Temperature is an average, not a total: a cup of tea and a bathtub can share a temperature while holding very different internal energy.

Common trap

Temperature is not “total internal energy”. Doubling the amount of matter doubles the energy at the same temperature.

02Temperature scales

Every scale needs two fixed, reproducible points. Celsius uses the ice and steam points of water at 1 atm; Kelvin shifts the zero to absolute zero.

ScaleReference pointsConversion
Celsius (°C)0 °C ice melt · 100 °C boil
Fahrenheit (°F)32 °F · 212 °F
Kelvin (K)Absolute zero = 0 K

Only Kelvin is an absolute scale: is the point where molecular translational motion stops, so ratios of temperatures are only meaningful in kelvin.

Why do we need kelvin for the gas laws?

Because the laws are proportional statements. only holds when is measured from absolute zero — at 0 °C a gas still has motion, so Celsius would make the ratio collapse at an arbitrary point.

Kelvin also keeps the kinetic formula honest: must reach zero when the motion does.
